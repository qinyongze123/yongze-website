The King's Trail: A Journey Within the Arctic Circle

During my two years of master's study I did go to many places and made many memories. Among them, the hiking trip on the King's Trail (Kungsleden) in northern Sweden is an especially unforgettable one.

The King's Trail is a classic hiking route in northern Sweden, about 440 kilometers long in total. Since the whole thing is far too long, a classmate and I, like most hikers, chose to traverse its most classic northern section (Abisko to Nikkaluokta), which is 105 kilometers long and takes about 6 days.

What Is Hiking

Hiking is basically people choosing to travel from one place to another for the sake of enjoyment. So in a broad sense, taking a stroll in a park or on a beach on a sunny afternoon counts as a kind of hiking. But when people are no longer satisfied with these short urban landscapes, they head to more sparsely populated outskirts and even the wilderness to hike, and the time may stretch from an afternoon to a day or two, or even a week. To make long hikes go well, these enthusiasts wear light clothing and sturdy hiking boots, and, to carry more food and water as well as bedding such as a tent and sleeping bag, hikers carry huge hiking backpacks to hold larger and heavier things. Considering that hikers can usually carry about 20% or even 25% of their own body weight, a multi-day hiking backpack can weigh 12–15kg, and to fit everything, its capacity may reach 40 to 60 liters. And to carry everything more easily, making hiking gear lightweight is very important; for example, hikers buy lighter sleeping bags and clothing in pursuit of a good "warmth-to-weight ratio".

Although the northern section of the King's Trail is also a multi-day hike, there are several cabins along the way for supplies and lodging. The cabins have no internet or running water, so you have to fetch water, burn firewood for heat, and use candles and gas for lighting. So we didn't bring a tent, only food and sleeping bags. But this route is inside the Arctic Circle, and we went in early March, when it was still very cold, with snow reaching half a meter deep on some stretches, so we needed to wear "snowshoes" to advance. These are wide, short snow boards with traction teeth on the bottom, used to reduce the pressure the hiker's feet exert on the snow while increasing friction for walking on the ice and snow surface. Strapping them onto ordinary outdoor hiking boots lets you hike directly through snow in winter.

Environment

The scenery of the northern section of the King's Trail is very beautiful. We hiked across vast snowfields and frozen lakes, across the tundra, with snow-covered peaks on both sides. Although long stretches of the path were covered in snow, from the ice surfaces, bridges and water points we could still tell that in summer this place must be a fertile land, nestled between mountains and surrounded by streams. This trip also passes Sweden's highest peak: Kebnekaise. The sunshine was abundant, and with the added help of reflection off the snow it even felt warm. At intervals along the way there are eye-catching trail markers (a metal pole with a red cross), used to point the way forward, and judging from how much of the metal pole is exposed, the snow in some stretches must have been over a meter deep.

The Dangers of Hiking

Hiking is dangerous. The danger lies in going to a sparsely populated place where, if an emergency occurs and you cannot deal with it, you end up in peril. The biggest danger on this King's Trail was hypothermia. The human body is a constant-temperature heat source, like a stove, continuously releasing heat to the outside. What we normally call cold is not the temperature of the body but the rate at which the body loses heat. When we feel cold, it means the body is losing heat quickly, and the skeletal muscles shiver to increase the heat supply and maintain body temperature. But when the body loses heat too fast, hypothermia occurs: body temperature cannot be maintained and is forced to fall, and the person becomes sluggish, confused, or even dies. Although we brought enough warm clothing, such clothing usually provides only limited warmth during the day. If clothes are damaged or soaked, if a foot steps into a stream, or if for any reason we fail to reach a cabin before sunset, it could bring big trouble. On this trip my gloves were of poor quality, and I could feel my hands never warming up. My classmate also had the experience of breaking through the ice layer and getting one foot wet in the stream. Overall, though, we were safe. In fact, on a later via ferrata trip in the Dolomites, the same two of us fell into real peril, which genuinely frightened me in hindsight, and I may talk about it in a later article. The Aurora

That afternoon we reached the cabin early, while it was still light. We were both exhausted, so we fell asleep on our beds early to rest. In the middle of it I was woken by the voices of other hikers, but kept sleeping in a half-dream. When I woke up, I saw two foreigners walking excitedly into the dorm. I lifted my head and asked them, "What's going on outside, what are those people talking about?" They happily explained to me that everyone had just seen the aurora, very clearly. I turned over and lay on the bed looking out the window, but there was no aurora at all. The sun had just set, and outside was just a vast expanse of white snow.

I was very disappointed. A few days earlier, when buying things at a cabin, the two of us had asked the shopkeeper about the aurora forecast. Since there is no internet on the King's Trail, the cabin shops use satellite communication to take payments, communicate and check weather forecasts. The shopkeeper pointed at some handwriting in a notebook and told us that in theory you can always see the aurora at night, but if there is even a bit of cloud in the sky, it will cover the aurora. The next few days would be clear, and might be the window for seeing the aurora on this hike. But it looked like we had just missed it.

So my classmate and I stopped sleeping. We got out of bed and went to the cabin's shared kitchen area, lit candles, and waited for the aurora while chatting. He thought that if there had been an aurora just now, there would surely be a chance to see it later. We chatted for a long time. We ate some of the communal food in the kitchen, I looked at the map on the wall, I studied the Swedish books on the bookshelf, I studied the terrain route map on the wall, and I looked at the water bottles left on the dining table by people before us who forgot to put them away, imagining what everyone had been doing earlier. Just as I was bored out of my mind, the aurora appeared.

At first, through the window of the shared kitchen, we saw wisps of aurora, a cluster here and a cluster there. The shape of the aurora was dynamic, like a fluttering ribbon. Sometimes it quietly appeared in some corner of the sky, then vanished after a while. Looking closely, the aurora is composed of thin segments of light running in one direction, with the texture of silk or fur. We were so excited that we decided to brave the low temperature and cold wind outside to go and look. And then I saw:

I didn't even want to put an aurora video here, because I don't want readers to think, "Oh, so that's what the aurora is like." In fact the aurora is not like it appears in the video. It is far more vivid and clear than what you see in the video, I just couldn't capture it well at night. The aurora really did fill the whole sky; it even lit up the ground. I had never seen anything like it. And it's certainly not a sight a video can present; only by traveling to the far north and observing with the naked eye can you appreciate the beauty of the aurora. I was shivering in the cold wind in a thin down jacket, and the fingers I used to take photos stiffened within seconds of coming out, but I just wanted to look a little longer. I even felt that whoever named the "aurora" went too far, because the word makes it sound so light and so natural. Although I had always heard of the "aurora", and learned that it was something like "high-speed particles captured by the magnetic field colliding with the atmosphere", before I truly saw it, I did not really understand what the aurora was.

Animals

Over the whole hike I didn't see many animals. At the very beginning I saw an eagle in the sky, and then for the next several days I saw no other living thing. I quickly accepted this fact, because I saw that the whole land was covered in ice and snow with almost no organic matter in sight, and at night the wind howled, and I couldn't imagine anything surviving in this environment. For that very reason, during the rest of the hike every animal I saw made me feel pleasantly surprised.

I saw a pure white bird, bigger than a sparrow but smaller than a pigeon, appearing in flocks on the snow. These birds were almost spherical in shape and looked very fluffy. If not for their black eyes, it would be hard to make them out against the snow. Unfortunately, I couldn't find the photos I took at the time; from the research I just did, they should be some kind of ptarmigan.

I also met a herd of reindeer. It was the second-to-last day, and after the long trek and low temperatures we were already very tired. I saw a big group of animals far in the distance. Because they were too far away, I couldn't identify them well. I said to my friend, "Could this be a pack of wolves? Have we run into wolves?" I couldn't help getting nervous, but looking more carefully, these animals felt larger than wolves, looking a bit like horses but smaller than horses. Only when we got really close did we figure out that it was a herd of deer. That night, chatting with other hikers, we learned they were reindeer, a smaller type of deer, the legendary mounts of Santa Claus, though some people said they had met elk.
